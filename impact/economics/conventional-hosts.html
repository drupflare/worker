The Conventional Hosts

  • Your cost: Cloudflare's published rates on your own account. free means the whole account fits inside the free plan.
  • Cheapest VPS: derived. Each priced provider's list price for the smallest box that meets the load in What A Conventional Host Needs, plus backups and any transfer overage, taking the lowest. It excludes the labour of running the server, which for most people is the largest real cost, and it excludes a CDN.
  • Most popular cloud: Amazon Lightsail, the VPS product of AWS. AWS is the most-used cloud among professional developers, at 45.9% in the 2025 Stack Overflow survey [20], and the largest by market share, at 28% in Synergy Research's second quarter of 2026 [21]. DigitalOcean, the most-used specialist VPS provider in that survey at 11.1%, is shown beside it in the price table.
  • Latency-matched VPS: derived (modelled). One origin in each of three regions (US East, Europe and Asia Pacific), each sized for a third of the traffic on DigitalOcean's plans, behind DigitalOcean's global load balancer at $15 a month [22]. It is what serving visitors from nearby costs conventionally. It is a floor: the database replica, file replication and cross-region transfer that multi-region architectures carry [23], [24] are not priced, and three regions still leave most visitors tens of milliseconds from an origin where the edge answers from their own colo.
  • Managed floor: Pantheon Basic at $500 a year billed annually, or $55 billed monthly [25]. published. The $41.67 a month in the tables is $500 divided by twelve, so it is derived. It is held flat because overage is not published in a form that can be quoted.
  • Energy: derived, from the energy model on Energy. The servers-not-built figure is modelled: the hardware share of the host, rounded down, with no manufacturing or recycling figure attached.

Google Cloud, Oracle Cloud, Kamatera, OVHcloud, Contabo, IONOS, Scaleway and Hetzner are not priced. Each lacked a complete USD list price for the components below on its own pages, or listed its cheapest plan as unavailable. Linode's per-plan backup price comes from its API [26].

What A Conventional Host Needs

A conventional price here is the bill for the hardware the traffic needs, and it moves with traffic. Peak load is five times the average, and every view is charged to the origin as a render. A render costs 44.7 ms of CPU, the capacity of one core of a Ryzen 9 9900X running the shipped nginx and PHP-FPM stack (What Running It Showed). The cores a box needs are the fewest whole cores that keep the p95 response inside a bound in an M/M/c queue, and never above a utilisation cap, at that peak rate.

Two bounds give two sizings. A viable box keeps the p95 under 60 seconds, the default read timeout of nginx and Apache [7], [27], [28], so no client or proxy gives up, with utilisation capped at 95%. Cloudflare's own cutoff is 125 seconds [29]. A matched box keeps the p95 under 0.8 seconds, the first-byte time web.dev rates good (poor starts at 1.8 seconds) [30], with utilisation capped at 80%. The caps are modelled, and the 80% matches Linode's guidance to stay below 80% sustained CPU on a shared plan [31]. A burstable plan has a lower baseline, such as the 10% of a vCPU on a t3.micro [32], but the average load here is a few percent of one core.

Memory sets the floor and does not grow with traffic. The Ubuntu 24.04 cloud image needs 1 GB of RAM for the operating system alone [33]. The shipped stack's nginx and 8 PHP-FPM children held 169 MB under the render load, and MariaDB's default InnoDB buffer pool is 128 MiB [34]. Together that is 1.29 GiB a box, so a 1 GiB plan is below the floor and 2 GiB is the smallest size that meets it. Drupal's own documentation gives 1 GB as the minimum for Composer [35], which is a build requirement and not a serving one. Disk is about 10 GB in use (the operating system, the 167 MB Drupal tree and the 5.8 MB database, modelled) against 25 GB on the smallest 2 GiB plans. Transfer is 166 KB a view, the first load of the demo site (66 requests, 17.2 KB of HTML, n=1), so 3 million views move about 498 GB. Every view is charged as a first load, and no CDN is assumed. The two sizings differ from there, and the gap widens with traffic: 9 vCPU against 11 at 100 million. The floor is the memory a box needs, and the traffic that follows moves the bill through the vCPU count above 9 million views and through transfer above a plan's included allowance.

What Running It Showed

The shipped nginx and PHP-FPM 8.5 stack, with opcache and the tracing JIT, on the same Drupal tree and SQLite database that drupflare serves, was run in one container image on two hosts and driven by the same generator from one machine, measured, by scripts/measure/floor-rig.ts. Every request is a render: a unique query string that misses nginx, the page cache and the dynamic page cache, checked on each response. Server time is nginx's own $request_time, so it carries no network.

host CPU a render renders a second at one core server time, one client
Ryzen 9 9900X, database on disk 44.1-46.4 ms 6.2 to 10.5 113 to 116 ms
same, database on tmpfs 44.8 ms 22.4 41 ms
Cloudflare Container, 1 vCPU 144.9 ms 6.6 144 ms
  • CPU: a render costs 44.1 to 46.4 ms of a Zen 5 core in all eight disk and tmpfs runs, across 1 and 2 CPUs, 8 to 32 children and 1 to 4 GiB limits, and the full-core capacity of 22.4 a second is 44.7 ms. An earlier rig gave 17.9 ms, which did not reproduce here, and the gap is unexplained. Cloudflare's container vCPU took 145 ms, so a slower core needs proportionally more of them.
  • Disk: with the database on disk a render also waits about 70 ms, and throughput stops near 10 a second however many cores it has: the 2-CPU run read 8.5. The container used about 0.45 of its core at that ceiling. Moving the database to tmpfs took one client from 113 ms to 41 ms and capacity to 22.4 a second, a full core. SQLite commits synchronously by default and every unique render writes cache rows, so this is that rig's commit latency, and MariaDB on a production disk was not measured. The cost model uses the CPU figure and no disk wait.
  • Queueing: on the tmpfs run the p95 was 190 ms at half of capacity and 272 ms at 80%, against 268 and 671 ms from an M/M/1 queue with that service time. The model's bound held with room, since render times vary less than an exponential. Errors appeared at 16 clients and in the 80% cell; the cause was not examined.
  • Memory: nginx plus PHP-FPM held 169 MB with 8 children, 227 MB with 16 and 342 MB with 32 under load, anonymous and shared memory from the container's cgroup, about 7.2 MB a child. A 1 GiB limit was reached 1,743 and 2,386 times, with 8 and 32 children, and killed nothing: anonymous memory stayed under 300 MB and the pressure was page cache. Per-child proportional memory could not be read, because the probe ran unprivileged.

The model charges 44.7 ms a render. That moves nothing at or below 3 million views, where a box is one vCPU, and above 9 million views it roughly doubles the vCPUs a load needs.
