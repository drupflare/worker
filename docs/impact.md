# Impact

What drupflare costs to run, in money, electricity, carbon and water, and how far each figure can be
trusted. The page leads with energy, then gives the models and measurements behind it, then carbon,
water and price.

## Measurement Labels

Every figure on this page carries one of four labels.

| label                | meaning                                                                 |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `measured`           | read off an instrument on real hardware, with an n and a spread         |
| `derived`            | arithmetic over measured inputs and published constants                 |
| `derived (modelled)` | arithmetic that also takes an input nobody has measured for a real site |
| `published`          | quoted from a named external source, cited where it appears             |

A figure keeps its label wherever it is repeated.

## Production Baseline

Enterprise Drupal runs oversupplied. Teams that put performance ahead of cost run high-availability,
multi-region deployments sized for peak, and those nodes draw their idle power around the clock. The
baseline is three regions, a high-availability web pair and database pair in each (12 nodes), 4-vCPU
nodes sized at 15% utilisation. Each node owns its vCPUs' share of a 640-thread host drawing 135 W
idle and 711 W at full load, at the 1.54 colocation PUE.

That deployment draws 224 kWh a year while idle-dominated. drupflare serving the same site at 1
million views a month draws 18.3 Wh a year, a saving of 99.9919%.

Idle energy of each deployment, and the saving of one drupflare site against it by views a month, the
worst case last. `derived (modelled)`, from `bun scripts/economics/perview.ts`.

| ------------------------------------------------ | -----------------------: | -------: | -------: | --------: | ---------: | ---------: |
| production: 3 regions, 12 nodes, 4 vCPU, 15%     |                    224.2 | 99.9998% | 99.9990% |  99.9919% |   99.9199% |   99.7601% |
| peak-sized: 3 regions, 12 nodes, 8 vCPU, 10%     |                    390.0 | 99.9999% | 99.9995% |  99.9953% |   99.9540% |   99.8621% |
| single-region HA: 1 region, 4 nodes, 4 vCPU, 15% |                     74.7 | 99.9993% | 99.9971% |  99.9756% |   99.7597% |   99.2802% |

The node count, the vCPU per node and the utilisation are modelled, because no source sizes a
production node. The topology follows Acquia's high-availability design, which spreads a site over several
availability zones of one region [4], and the AWS reference architecture for Drupal, whose subnets
sit in two or three zones [5]. Neither states instance sizes. The 135 W and 711 W server power, the 320 cores and the
640 threads are the SPECpower result [1]. The PUE is 1.54 for the conventional host and 1.15 for
Cloudflare's hyperscale facilities, both Uptime Institute averages [6]. A VPS vCPU is a hardware thread,
so a node owns its vCPUs over 640 of the host.

drupflare's busy core is a physical core, because each Workers isolate thread runs on one. Its energy
is CPU time on the traffic mix at 2.22 W a core, charged under [Watts For A Busy
Core](#watts-for-a-busy-core), with logged-in views at the compiled-plan cost. [Energy Per
View](#energy-per-view) has the mix and the plan.

The saving is the idle term. These nodes are provisioned for peak, so almost all of their energy is
spent waiting. A site that sleeps between requests holds no compute and spends energy only while a
view, a render or a cold boot executes. A site kept warm also pays for its warming and holds its
memory, which [Operating States](#operating-states) prices. Per request the two runtimes are at
parity, as the measurements below show, so none of this comes from PHP running faster.

### Watts For A Busy Core

The 2.22 W charge is the host's 711 W at full load over its 320 cores. That is the full-load end of
the measured curve, so it is a floor: a host that runs below full load spends more per busy
core-second. From the same SPECpower result [1], `derived`:

| host load | watts per busy core | against the model | linear model understates the draw by |
| --------: | ------------------: | ----------------: | -----------------------------------: |
|      100% |                2.22 |             1.00x |                                1.00x |
|       50% |                2.91 |             1.31x |                                1.10x |
|       30% |                3.76 |             1.69x |                                1.17x |
|       15% |                5.88 |             2.65x |                                1.27x |
|       10% |                8.00 |             3.60x |                                1.33x |

The idle and full-load points are the result's own, and the 256 W at 10% load and 466 W at 50% are
read from it. Charging 8.0 W a busy core raises drupflare's 0.525 Wh a year at 10,000 views to 1.89 Wh
and leaves the saving above 99.98% against one small VPS. It cancels in every per-view ratio below,
because both sides are priced at the same watts per core.

The conventional figures carry the opposite error. The model interpolates linearly between idle and
peak, which gives 221 W for a host at 15% load where the measured curve gives 282 W, so every
conventional figure here is about 27% low and the saving is understated. SPECpower's load is a share
of throughput rather than of CPU utilisation, so read the 27% as a direction and not a correction.

### The Worst Case

The last row of the production table is the smallest conventional deployment there is: one small VPS
of two vCPUs, a 320th of a host at 15% utilisation, no redundancy and one region. It draws 9.34 kWh a
year, and the saving holds there too: 99.8045% at a million views a month and 94.2413% at 30 million,
`derived (modelled)`. The utilisation and host share are chosen to be generous to the VPS, so this is
the lowest saving the model produces, not a typical one. The sections below use this deployment
wherever they quote a single-site VPS.

## Operating States

A site is either asleep or kept warm, and a third accounting charges the kept-warm site for the memory
it holds. The headline above is the sleeping state, the ceiling on efficiency. The last row of the
second table is the most pessimistic bound the model carries; it is not a floor under every possible
state.

| state                     | what selects it                                                                                           | an editor's first request after idle                           |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| asleep                    | the free plan's default, where the thermal policy leaves a quiet site alone, or `SITE_WARM=0` on any plan | a cold interpreter boot, 2 to 4 s; a cached page is unaffected |
| kept warm                 | the paid plan's default: the alarm re-arms every 8 s and the object never hibernates                      | 156 to 371 ms                                                  |
| kept warm, memory charged | an accounting of the row above, not a configuration                                                       | 156 to 371 ms                                                  |

The latencies are the measured warming curve in the [configuration reference](configuration.md).
Choosing between the first two trades cost and energy against an editor's cold start, and both are
supported. A thousand sites at 10,000 views each cost $5.80 a month asleep and $350.65 kept warm on
one account, $0.0058 and $0.351 a site, because the warming chain's 10,800 firings and 10,896 rows a
site a day are billed against an allowance that is account-wide; [What It Costs To
Run](#what-it-costs-to-run) has the tables.

Energy a year of one site in each state, `derived (modelled)`, from `bun scripts/economics/states.ts`.

At 10,000,000 views the same rows run from 179.5 Wh asleep to 924 Wh in the last row, which is 99.92%
to 99.59% against production and 98.08% to 90.11% against one VPS. Every row saves more than 99.5%
against production and more than 90% against the worst case. The compound bound adds the full object
budget, the warming CPU at the billed wall time and five cold encounters a day. It is not a state a
site sits in: an object that stays warm does not meet idle cold boots, so those encounters come from
outside causes such as an eviction, a deploy or a replaced isolate.

- **Cold encounters:** a sensitivity with no measured rate. A cold encounter is a boot plus a first
  render with the cache bins empty, 1,264 ms plus 2,127 ms of CPU, both measured. How many a site
  meets is the number of editor sessions that find the object cold, and cached anonymous views never
  boot PHP. `coldOfTraffic` is the instrument for the rate and no deployed site has reported a
  reading, so the headline carries none. At 10,000 views, five a day moves the saving from 99.9998% to
  99.9978%.
- **Warming CPU:** bounded, not read. A firing bills 23.3 ms of wall time (n=116 on two deployed
  objects) and its CPU time has not been read. The first warm row charges all of the wall time as
  CPU, an upper bound; the second charges a tenth.
- **Memory:** the memory rows charge a kept-warm object for the memory it holds, as accounting bounds.
  A booted interpreter takes about 165 to 175 MiB of an object's 195 MiB budget, measured on a paid
  object with a build whose linear memory started at 80 MiB; the shipping build starts at 64 MiB,
  which can only lower it. A large site such as farmOS holds 196 to 201 MB warm. The first row
  charges 170 MiB and the second the full 195 MiB, the limit at which the platform resets an object
  and not a measured footprint for every site. The memory of a warm object is a plateau: an
  interpreter's linear memory is reused after a drop and keeps the largest size any interpreter
  reached, so a kept-warm object holds its high-water mark until its incarnation ends. 170 MiB of the
  384 GiB host is 0.0432%, charged at the host's 135 W idle draw and the 1.15 PUE: 589 Wh a year. That
  assumes the host is provisioned for memory. Cloudflare does not publish how objects are packed, and
  if the memory sits in DRAM that is powered anyway the marginal charge is zero. A site that sleeps
  ends its incarnation after ten idle seconds, and a cached page is served without booting the
  interpreter, so it holds the interpreter's memory only while it renders. The sleeping rows carry no
  memory charge.

## Where The Saving Comes From

The saving has two sources, and neither depends on PHP running faster.

- **Idle capacity:** conventional multi-tenant hosting for small sites is memory-bound. Packed at
  1,000 sites of 1 GB on a 1.5 TB two-socket host, the fleet runs its processors near zero and draws
  about 1,826 kWh a year at 10,000 views a site, 1,822 of it idle (99.8%). It buys processors it never
  uses and powers them continuously. A sleeping site consumes energy only while it executes.
- **Invalidation:** a page store invalidates on content change, while a CDN expires on a clock.
  `cfw_page` carries a `stale_at` column set when a save invalidates a tag, so a cached page survives
  until the content behind it changes. A CDN in front of a conventional origin re-renders every path,
  at every edge location, once per TTL whether or not anything changed. Modelled at 20 million views
  per site per month with five saves a day, that is 28.34% of requests rendering for a CDN and origin
  against 0.004% for drupflare, 186,371 renders a day against 25. A per-location cache miss reaches
  the page store, which answers without PHP.

### The Render Fraction

`derived (modelled)`. Models of edge-cached hosting often assume a flat share of requests that
render. The share depends on traffic:

```
renders = sum over paths of min(requests, window / TTL)   per path, per edge location
          + saves x pages invalidated per save
```

The floor does not depend on traffic, so the fraction falls as traffic rises. It is a property of a
site's content-to-traffic ratio, and a single number for a whole fleet is wrong in both directions.
For a CDN and origin a common 1% assumption is first reached at roughly 723 million views per site
per month, far above the range these models cover.

drupflare has no TTL term and no location term. Its renders are saves times the pages each save
invalidates, 5 for an ordinary node save, so at five saves a day a site renders about 25 pages a day
whatever its traffic. The fraction is 7.6% at 10,000 views a month, 1% at about 76,000 and 0.004% at
20 million. A save the main menu references invalidates every cached page, 34 on the measured site,
and is the pessimistic arm.

For a CDN and origin the edge-location count is the largest term: at 20 million views it spans 4.38%
at one location to 62.91% at fifty. A measurement taken from a single source address reads a floor,
because a one-address generator drives one edge location while real visitors spread across many.

`scripts/measure/render-fraction.ts` computes it, with each input named as measured or modelled.

## Where The Figures Come From

Three deployments appear on this page, and they are not equally observable.

| deployment                           | label                          | what it answers                            |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| conventional VPS, nginx plus PHP-FPM | `measured`                     | joules per cached serve and per render     |
| drupflare on standalone `workerd`    | `measured`                     | the same, for this runtime, on one counter |
| drupflare on Cloudflare              | measured CPU, `derived` joules | the workload; joules need published W/core |

Rows one and two run on the same machine against the same RAPL counter, so the runtime comparison
under Energy Per Request is a measurement. Row three stays derived: Cloudflare exposes no energy
counter to a container and no energy field in its analytics, so its CPU time is read and its wattage
is an allocation.

### The Two Hosts

Joules come from one machine and Cloudflare CPU time from another, because only one of the two can
report energy at all. Both were probed before either was used.

|                | Cloudflare Container             | `paisley-park`           |
| -------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| kind           | Firecracker microVM              | bare metal               |
| processor      | AMD EPYC, Zen 4                  | AMD Ryzen 9 9900X, Zen 5 |
| cores visible  | 1 vCPU                           | 12 cores, 24 threads     |
| cores enforced | 1/16 vCPU on the `lite` type     | all                      |
| memory         | 256 MiB                          | 30 GB                    |
| kernel         | `6.18.36-cloudflare-firecracker` | Ubuntu 24.04, 6.8        |
| discrete GPU   | none                             | RTX 4070, 11.1 W idle    |
| power supply   | not visible                      | 750 W, 80 Plus Bronze    |

The container runs as root with the full capability set, no seccomp filter, a writable `/sys` and
`/dev/cpu/0/msr` present, and still has no energy counter: `/sys/class/powercap` is absent, no
`power` PMU is registered, the AMD RAPL MSRs answer `I/O error` and the Intel ones return a constant
zero. `nomodule` on the kernel command line means no driver can be inserted to change that.

`paisley-park` has the counter the comparison needs. `/sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0` exposes
`package-0` and `core` domains with a 65,532,610,987 uJ range. The file is root-only by default
under the Platypus mitigation, and a session-scoped permission change makes it readable.

Two readings from the container. Its `lite` instance type is enforced outside the guest's view: the
guest sees one full vCPU and `nr_throttled` stays at zero, while `/proc/stat` reports 48% of wall
clock as steal time, which is how the 1/16 share arrives. And its own accounting sits between
Cloudflare's two published datasets for the same run, at 11.06 CPU-seconds against 5.59 reported for
the workload and 22.10 for the workload plus its micro-VM sandbox.

The container measures CPU time, memory, disk and network, and can never measure joules.
`paisley-park` measures processor-package joules for both runtimes, drupflare under standalone
`workerd` and the VPS stack, and cannot measure the wall, because a wall meter needs physical access.
Neither host can measure drupflare on Cloudflare's own hardware. That side is `cpuTime` from a
deployed worker priced at watts per core, and the per-view figures price both arms from CPU time at
the same watts, so a measured joule is never set against a derived one.

## Energy Per Request

`measured`. RAPL package and core domains on a bare-metal AMD Ryzen 9 9900X, load generated off-box,
arms rotated per round, idle subtracted, n=5 per rung with no sample discarded. Both stacks run on
the same machine against the same counter in one interleaved run: the shipped `docker/vps.yml`
(nginx plus PHP 8.5 FPM with opcache and JIT) and drupflare under standalone `workerd serve`, each
capped at 3 CPUs by cgroup.

The cap is the VPS stack's total, `vps-php` at 2 plus `vps-web` at 1. It is read back from the running
containers as a `cpu.max` of `200000 100000` and `100000 100000`, because a `deploy.resources` block
is honoured by some Compose versions and ignored by others.

### A Per-Request Figure Needs Its Rate

The same arm reads 279, 202 and 131 mJ per request at 29, 57 and 114 requests per second. The decline
is the processor package leaving its idle C-states, a step that costs the same whether the window
carried 300 requests or 1,100. Idle subtraction cannot remove it, because it is absent from the idle
arm by definition. A per-request figure quoted without its rate is that fixed cost divided by
whatever throughput the run reached.

A fixed-plus-marginal fit does not fix this. The marginal cost itself falls with rate, 123 mJ per
additional request between 29 and 57 per second against 60 mJ between 57 and 114, so the curve is
concave and a fitted slope describes the spacing of the rungs. Both arms are compared at the same
request count instead, interpolating between measured rungs.

`measured`. Package energy over idle per 10-second window, drupflare against the VPS stack at the same
request count. Two independent runs, n=5 and n=3 per rung, 104 samples with zero failed requests. The
second ran on a freshly provisioned site.

| cached |               1,101 |                   154.60 |             147.28 | 1.050x |
| cached |                 532 |                   105.53 |             100.68 | 1.048x |
| cached |               1,043 |                   145.51 |             144.62 | 1.006x |

The two runtimes cost the same energy per request to within about 13%, on identical hardware, on both
the cached tier and a full render. PHP compiled to wasm and executed inside a Durable Object is
neither cheaper nor meaningfully dearer per unit of work than native PHP-FPM.

That agrees with the same-machine timing: a re-render on a tag-invalidated page is 24 ms on the VPS
against 30 on drupflare, and a page whose dynamic page cache is still warm is 24 against 22.

Throughput differs. On this host the VPS stack saturates near 525 requests per second and the
drupflare arm near 220, because a site is one single-threaded Durable Object while the VPS has 32
PHP-FPM children. The replica pool addresses that and was not enabled for this run. Both arms were
driven well below their ceilings, so the energy figures measure work and not queueing.

### Logged-In Views

`measured`. A logged-in administrator's view of `/admin/content` is answered by the compiled-plan tier
at 2.1 ms of CPU against 19.9 ms natively, and costs 503 mJ against 751 mJ per view on the same
machine, 33% less. Conditions: RAPL package on `paisley-park`, idle subtracted, 40 offered views a
second, n=6 to 7 rounds per cell, drupflare on a `bastion` node under a 3-CPU cgroup against the
shipped nginx and PHP-FPM stack at 3 CPUs.

answers in 12 ms at the median and costs no Durable Object invocation at all.

## Energy Per View

The figures above are per render or per cached serve. A visitor sees a view, and what a view costs
depends on how often it renders, so this section prices the mix. These are the marginal energy of
serving a view, with no idle power in them, unlike the totals in [Energy Savings](#energy-savings).
Everything here is `derived (modelled)` unless marked, from `bun scripts/economics/perview.ts`.

The lifetimes favour the caches: 24 h is the longest modelled. drupflare still renders less often than every one of them because it never renders on
a clock. What that saves per view depends on what a view costs when it does not render, which the rest

### Energy On The Traffic Mix

`config/traffic.yml` weights 82% anonymous reads of an existing page, 9.5% uncached anonymous views,
3% signed-in readers, 3% editors in the admin interface and 2.5% signed-in users on their own page.
Logged-in views are included. A conventional host renders every logged-in view, because its cache is
bypassed for a session; drupflare answers them from the compiled plan. Both sides are priced from CPU
time at the same watts per core and PUE, with the opponent's CPU scaled to the deployed render, so one
instrument charges both. The drupflare row is the figure the headline and the production table use.

The 9.5% uncached share sets the mix and is not a render count. A render is priced from the page-store
model for drupflare, saves times the pages a save invalidates, and from each opponent's lifetime model
for the others; a view that a store or a cache already holds costs a hit. The weight would matter if
every such view were a PHP render on drupflare. That adds 0.095 of the 149.2 mJ gap between a render
(153.8 mJ) and a hit (4.6 mJ), 14.2 mJ a view, and a native opponent that rendered them all adds 8.7
mJ. At a million views that is 19.7 mJ for drupflare against 16.6 mJ for the 24-hour shield, so the
lead depends on those views not rendering. `config/traffic.yml` calls them the uncached tail, a first
visit or a purged path, and no deployed site has reported what share of them reaches PHP.

### The Two Penalties

drupflare pays two costs that a native host does not:

- **Wasm render:** 1.65 times the CPU of a native one, `measured`, at 32.5 ms against 19.7 ms for the
  same page on the same machine. At the same watts that is 153.8 mJ against 92.9 mJ per render.
- **Cached hit:** 4.6 mJ against 1.9 mJ for an nginx hit, `derived` on one CPU basis. A view that
  reaches drupflare is charged the front Worker and the object, 1.8 ms of CPU, while nginx answers a
  hit from its own cache.

Rendering less often outweighs both up to a point the model prices. An nginx-hit opponent costs more
per anonymous view than drupflare once it renders on more than 3.0% of views, because renders at that
rate repay the 2.7 mJ gap on the hit. Stock nginx and the one-hour shield sit above that line at 100,000
and 1,000,000 views a month, and the mix table above shows the result: 2.5 to 12 times the energy per
view. The same comparison on anonymous views alone, `derived (modelled)`:

| -------------------------------------- | -----: | ------: | --------: | ---------: | ---------: |
| drupflare                              |  15.95 |    5.73 |      4.71 |       4.61 |       4.60 |
| stock nginx, 5 min, no purge           |  92.95 |   80.33 |     43.49 |       9.73 |       4.51 |
| one shield, 1 h, tag purge, nginx hit  |  84.72 |   41.05 |      8.54 |       2.52 |       2.07 |

add 5.9 mJ per view to the opponent and 1.2 to drupflare, which renders none of them when the plan
holds.

### The Closest Opponent

The closest opponent is a shielded 24 h tag-purged cache, which uses 1.45 times drupflare's energy per
view at a million views and 1.42 times from ten million up. Its lifetime is the longest modelled, so it is
chosen to favour the opponent. A shield collapses
the per-location expiry to one fill, and a 24 h lifetime with tag purge leaves it almost nothing to
render. Two further assumptions in the model favour it:

- The model has no CDN eviction term. A real cache drops cold pages under memory pressure and each
  location fills cold, while a page store that persists on disk does not.
- The opponent's hit is priced as an origin nginx hit, 1.9 mJ, because no source publishes the cost of
  a CDN hit. Costing the hit at zero is a floor and gives 1.10 to 1.14 times.

saving where renders dominate. Through 150 million views per site per month the quoted arm is

first falls below 90% at 23.9 million views per site per month, below 75% at 88.7 million and below
50% at 364.4 million.

The saving never reaches zero in this model, and the floor comes from an assumption. Once both arms
are CPU-bound, per-request energy is at parity, so the remaining gap is the facility overhead the
model assumes, 1.15 PUE for a hyperscale operator against 1.54 for colocation. That caps the saving
near 1 - 1.15 / 1.54, or 25.3%, and the model reaches 25.34% at a trillion views. An opponent hosted
at hyperscale PUE would lose that floor, leaving only the render-fraction difference.

accounting, and charging three requests per page view, drupflare's attributed carbon crosses one
site's share of shared hosting at about 793,000 views per month. Below that the saving is 87% to
99%. Above it the saving inverts.

### What A Conventional Host Needs

A conventional price here is the bill for the hardware the traffic needs, and it moves with traffic.
Peak load is five times the average, and every view is charged to the origin as a render. A render
costs 44.7 ms of CPU, the capacity of one core of a Ryzen 9 9900X running the shipped nginx and
PHP-FPM stack (What Running It Showed). The cores a box needs are the fewest whole cores that keep the p95 response inside a bound in
an M/M/c queue, and never above a utilisation cap, at that peak rate.

Two bounds give two sizings. A viable box keeps the p95 under 60 seconds, the default read timeout
of nginx and Apache [7], [27], [28], so no client or proxy gives up, with utilisation capped at 95%.
Cloudflare's own cutoff is 125 seconds [29]. A matched box keeps the p95 under 0.8 seconds, the
first-byte time web.dev rates good (poor starts at 1.8 seconds) [30], with utilisation capped at 80%.
The caps are modelled, and the 80% matches Linode's guidance to stay below 80% sustained CPU on a
shared plan [31]. A burstable plan has a lower baseline, such as the 10% of a vCPU on a `t3.micro`
[32], but the average load here is a few percent of one core.

Memory sets the floor and does not grow with traffic. The Ubuntu 24.04 cloud image needs 1 GB of RAM
for the operating system alone [33]. The shipped stack's nginx and 8 PHP-FPM children held 169 MB
under the render load, and MariaDB's default InnoDB buffer pool is 128 MiB [34]. Together that is
1.29 GiB a box, so a 1 GiB plan is below the floor and 2 GiB is the smallest size that meets it.
Drupal's own documentation gives 1 GB as the minimum for Composer [35], which is a build requirement
and not a serving one. Disk is about 10 GB in use (the operating system, the 167 MB Drupal tree and
the 5.8 MB database, modelled) against 25 GB on the smallest 2 GiB plans. Transfer is 166 KB a view,
the first load of the demo site (66 requests, 17.2 KB of HTML, n=1), so 3 million views move about
498 GB. Every view is charged as a first load, and no CDN is assumed. What a conventional host needs at each

### What Running It Showed

The shipped nginx and PHP-FPM 8.5 stack, with opcache and the tracing JIT, on the same Drupal tree and
SQLite database that drupflare serves, was run in one container image on two hosts and driven by the
same generator from one machine, `measured`, by `scripts/measure/floor-rig.ts`. Every request is a
render: a unique query string that misses nginx, the page cache and the dynamic page cache, checked on
each response. Server time is nginx's own `$request_time`, so it carries no network.

| host                            | CPU a render | renders a second at one core | server time, one client |
| ------------------------------- | -----------: | ---------------------------: | ----------------------: |
| Ryzen 9 9900X, database on disk | 44.1-46.4 ms |                  6.2 to 10.5 |           113 to 116 ms |
| same, database on tmpfs         |      44.8 ms |                         22.4 |                   41 ms |
| Cloudflare Container, 1 vCPU    |     144.9 ms |                          6.6 |                  144 ms |

- **CPU:** a render costs 44.1 to 46.4 ms of a Zen 5 core in all eight disk and tmpfs runs, across 1
  and 2 CPUs, 8 to 32 children and 1 to 4 GiB limits, and the full-core capacity of 22.4 a second is
  44.7 ms. The model used 17.9 ms from an earlier rig, and that figure did not reproduce here. The gap
  is unexplained. Cloudflare's container vCPU took 145 ms, so a slower core needs proportionally more
  of them.
- **Disk:** with the database on disk a render also waits about 70 ms, and throughput stops near 10 a
  second however many cores it has: the 2-CPU run read 8.5. The container used about 0.45 of its core
  at that ceiling. Moving the database to tmpfs took one client from 113 ms to 41 ms and capacity to
  22.4 a second, a full core. SQLite commits synchronously by default and every unique render writes
  cache rows, so this is that rig's commit latency, and MariaDB on a production disk was not
  measured. The cost model uses the CPU figure and no disk wait.
- **Queueing:** on the tmpfs run the p95 was 190 ms at half of capacity and 272 ms at 80%, against
  268 and 671 ms from an M/M/1 queue with that service time. The model's bound held with room, since
  render times vary less than an exponential. Errors appeared at 16 clients and in the 80% cell; the
  cause was not examined.
- **Memory:** nginx plus PHP-FPM held 169 MB with 8 children, 227 MB with 16 and 342 MB with 32 under
  load, anonymous and shared memory from the container's cgroup, about 7.2 MB a child. A 1 GiB limit
  was reached 1,743 and 2,386 times, with 8 and 32 children, and killed nothing: anonymous memory
  stayed under 300 MB and the pressure was page cache. Per-child proportional memory could not be
  read, because the probe ran unprivileged.

The model now charges 44.7 ms a render. That moves nothing at or below 3 million views, where a box
is one vCPU, and above 9 million views it roughly doubles the vCPUs a load needs.

### What The Price Column Does Not Buy

A monthly price buys capacity from one place, and this page does not measure capacity beyond the
sizing above. It does not buy the latency profile: the same workload measures 100.2 ms on a VPS
against 8.9 ms on drupflare once an 82 ms round trip is injected, because one serves from a region
and the other from the visitor's colo. It does not buy the tail either, where the VPS p50 runs from
59 ms at 4 clients to 182 ms at 96 while drupflare holds. The latency-matched column prices the first
of those; nothing here prices the second.

### The Shape Of The Bills

The two bills differ in shape more than in total. A conventional host charges per site per month
whether or not anyone visits, and for hardware that grows with traffic; drupflare charges for traffic
served, against an allowance that costs nothing until it is crossed. The comparison therefore widens
with the number of idle sites and narrows with traffic. For a single site at ten million views a
month drupflare costs $5.15, the $5 subscription plus $0.15 of usage, against $10.50 for the
cheapest VPS, and the latency-matched host is 11.3 times dearer.

## What This Does Not Claim

- **No per-tenant Cloudflare wattage.** It is not observable. Probing a deployed Cloudflare Container
  found no energy counter by any of the six routes a Linux process has, and no energy field exists
  anywhere in the account's analytics schema. The Cloudflare side of every figure above is an
  allocation over measured workload and published hardware.
- **No cost for custom domains.** The bills above are Workers account bills. A site on its own domain
  through Cloudflare for SaaS adds $0.10 a hostname a month past the first 100 [40], so 1,000 of them
  add $90 a month and take the asleep bill at 10,000 views from $5.80 to $95.80. How a fleet's domains
  attach decides whether that applies, and `scripts/economics/pricing.ts` takes the count.
- **No consolidated-host bill.** The conventional bills price one origin a site. A consolidated host
  pays for memory alone at least $5.00 a GiB a month at the cheapest sourced plan: $5,000 for 1,000
  sites at 1 GiB each, $1,250 at 0.25 GiB and $500 at 0.1 GiB, before CPU, disk, transfer and
  backups. A bill of $350.65 buys 71.8 MiB of memory a site across 1,000 sites and $5.80 buys 1.2 MiB,
  so a consolidated host undercuts the kept-warm bill only if a site fits in about 72 MiB. No source
  gives a Drupal site's memory on a shared host.
- **No claim that 1,000 sites replace 1,000 servers.** VPS providers and managed platforms already
  consolidate, so the comparison is against hosting that has captured most of the multi-tenancy
  saving.
- **Conventional prices are sized list prices.** They exclude labour, a CDN, the database and file
  replication of a multi-region origin and cross-region transfer, and they price a box from documented
  minimums and a measured stack. The stack was measured with SQLite and not MariaDB, on one machine
  and on a Cloudflare container, and no source gives PHP-FPM memory per child for Drupal.
- **The energy worst case and the priced box differ in size.** The energy model charges a VPS a 320th
  of a host, two vCPUs. The cost model prices one vCPU and 2 GiB, which is a 192nd of a 384 GiB host by
  memory, so the energy charge is at most low for the box priced.
- **Pantheon as a price, not an energy baseline.** It is already multi-tenant, and how many machines
  it runs, how well utilised they are and how much of that would persist without a given workload
  are all unobservable from outside.
- **No market-share multiplier.** A CMS's share of websites is a count of sites and not a quantity of
  compute. It changes the addressable population and transfers no measurement.
- **Incremental energy is not a hosting total.** The rig produces idle-subtracted workload energy. A
  continuously provisioned server also pays its idle, and the equivalent allocation on a shared edge
  fleet cannot be measured. [Operating States](#operating-states) brackets it from zero to a
  memory-share bound; neither end is a reading.
- **No allocation for Cloudflare's own fleet utilisation.** The 2.22 W a busy core is the full-load
  floor, and [Watts For A Busy Core](#watts-for-a-busy-core) shows what a lower host load would
  charge. At 10,000 views that leaves the saving above 99.98% against one VPS, and it moves no
  per-view ratio, since both sides use the same figure.
- **No cold-encounter reading.** The headline carries none, and no deployed site has reported
  `coldOfTraffic`. The sensitivity rows are assumptions about how many editor sessions find an object
  cold.
- **No price on the hostname lookup.** A request through the production hostname resolves the site
  from a key-value mapping, memoised for a minute, and its CPU time has not been read. Its wall time
  is 4 ms warm and 46 to 140 ms in a colo that has not seen the key, and CPU time excludes the wait.
  The closest per-view cell has room for about 0.3 ms of extra CPU on every view against a free hit
  and 0.9 ms against an nginx hit, at a million views a month and above.
- **No price on the shell tier, replica lanes or the plan's compile.** A logged-in view that misses
  the compiled plan can be answered from a stored shell, which renders a fragment instead of the
  page. Lanes copy writes to every object of a busy site. The plan compiles from three agreeing
  sessions. None has an energy class here, and [How The Lead Moves](#how-the-lead-moves) charges a
  missed plan as a render.
- **Institutional rows use an assumed workload.** The page views come from the case studies. The
  logged-in share, the saves a day and the pages a save invalidates are the model's, not theirs.

```sh
bun scripts/measure/vps-energy.ts run --ssh="$HOST" --target="$VPS_ADDR" --target2="$DRUPFLARE_ADDR" --ladder=2,4,8 --n=5
bun scripts/measure/render-fraction.ts
bun scripts/economics/perview.ts
bun scripts/economics/states.ts
bun scripts/economics/fleet.ts
bun scripts/economics/comparison.ts --md
bun scripts/economics/comparison.ts --crossover
bun scripts/economics/comparison.ts --sites=426 --views=82160
```

The first command needs a bare-metal x86 host with readable RAPL counters and a load generator on a
different machine. A virtual machine cannot substitute: a Cloudflare Container exposes no energy
counter at all, and a mainstream KVM guest exposes a `power` PMU that reads a constant zero, which a
tool reports as 0 W. `--ladder` is required for a per-request figure. One rate cannot separate the
cost of a request from the cost of the package being awake, so the two arms are read at the same
request count.

Conventional host prices live in `scripts/economics/hosting-catalog.json`: every plan and rate carries
the provider page it was read from and the line quoted from it, and `tests/node/hosting-catalog.spec.ts`
fails on one without a source. The economic models live in `scripts/economics/`. Every measured input they take is in one file with
its provenance and the workload it was taken under, because a render cost measured with cold caches
and a render cost measured on the invalidation path differ by 35x and are not substitutable.
